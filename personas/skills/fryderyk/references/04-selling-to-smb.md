# 04 — Selling to SMB

**Load when:** the question is about selling B2B to small/medium businesses, why SaaS playbooks don't apply, understanding the buyer's reality, the four-stakeholder model.

## The frame: forget the SaaS playbook

The single biggest mistake people make selling to SMB hospitality: they reach for a copy-pasted SaaS sales playbook. Slack's. Calendly's. HubSpot's. Whatever the latest VC-funded sales motion is.

It doesn't work. Not because the playbook is bad — it's great, for what it was built for. It was built for SaaS sold to office workers checking email every 5 minutes, sitting on LinkedIn, evaluating Teams vs Slack with a small group, signing on a credit card.

That is not the SMB hospitality buyer.

Why this matters: most of the foodtech graveyard from 2020–2024 was platforms that raised millions, hired SaaS experts, ran the SaaS playbook, and crashed into the wall. The playbook can't fail in their model — so when it fails, the conclusion was "the market doesn't work". It does. The playbook doesn't.

You also can't copy the Uber Eats / Glovo / Wolt merchant-acquisition playbook. Those work because the proposition is *idiot-proof*: "join, you only pay if you get an order, we take 30%". One-line value prop, near-zero risk, instant upside boost. That's not a SaaS playbook either — that's marketplace acquisition. You're selling something more complicated than a marketplace, but to the same merchant.

You have to build your own playbook. That's bad news — it's hard, slow, and lonely.

It's also good news — because it's hard, once you crack it you have *cholerne competitive advantage and defensibility*. You play your own game.

## The four-stakeholder model

Before designing anything — product, sales pitch, onboarding — map the stakeholders. For a café-bakery, there are four:

1. **The end customer.** Walks in, buys coffee, walks out. Probably half-asleep, late, in the rain. The line is behind them; the queue is impatient. The transaction must be **fast**. Their UX is not "browse, evaluate, select" — it's "I need this now". This is a *fully offline experience*. Unlike a supermarket or Zara, there is no built-in moment to collect data.

2. **The frontline (barista, server).** Maybe in their first week. Maybe a colleague didn't show. The POS is misbehaving. The job for them is often a casual one — you can't expect them to think like an owner. They want to get through the shift. Anything you add to their workflow had better be lighter than what was there before, or they will work around it.

3. **The manager.** Wants the customer happy, the staff functioning, the day running. Their KPI is "no fires today". Anything that creates new fires is a no, even if it's strategically valuable.

4. **The owner.** At the end of the day this is who pays for the system, and who carries the survival mode. They want revenue, margin, retention. They want to understand what's happening in their business with data they don't have today. They are also drowning in operational fires, so they will not give your product a 30-minute deep evaluation under good conditions.

Your job: deliver enough **upside** for each of the four to say yes, or at minimum minimise the downside. Any one of them killing the deal is a kill. The frontline ignoring the system kills it just as effectively as the owner saying no.

## The "mindfack" reality of the owner

This is the hardest thing to internalise if you've never run one of these businesses. The owner is balancing two completely different mental modes simultaneously:

- **Creative / artistic mode.** They run a hospitality business. They have to constantly create the "wow" experience, even if the wow is just a great latte and a kind word. They can't stop doing this. The moment they do, the business starts dying.
- **Operator / accountant mode.** They have to think about cups, beans, wine, sandwiches, staff schedules, broken taps, no-show staff, power outages, supplier price changes, tax changes, courier fees, weather, public transport strikes affecting footfall.

The "fixed costs" in this business are variable week-to-week. One quote from an experienced operator: "this is the only business where costs that are defined as fixed are variable. Every single week."

This means the owner's headspace is permanently full. Not "I'm too busy this week" — structurally full, all the time, even when business is good. The deficit isn't only time — it's **head space**.

Now picture: you're cold-pitching them a SaaS. You're bombarding them with one of a hundred similar-sounding solutions, all promising more sales, better retention, lower costs. They have no slack to evaluate, no slack to compare, no slack to even understand the difference between yours and the next person's.

## What this means for how you sell

Empathy is not a nice-to-have. It's the lens through which everything else works.

- **Don't waste their headspace.** Every message should be short, specific to *them*, anchored in something they already do.
- **Don't pretend the system is the system that will save them.** They've heard that 50 times. Anchor your offer in something they already understand (see `07-demo-and-storytelling.md` — the stamp-card-as-base story).
- **Use their language, not yours.** "CRM", "loyalty system", "engagement metrics" — these are *your* words. They have their words: "stałych klientów", "klientów co wracają", "promocja na kawę i kanapkę".
- **Lower the headspace cost of adoption.** Make it easy to evaluate, easy to install, easy to use. Stress this in every interaction. See `11-onboarding-emphasis.md`.
- **Respect their non-work time.** When they're on vacation, leave them alone (see `09-followups-and-closing.md`).

## What he says about the operator

"Szapała ba do wszystkich ludzi, którym poszło w gastronomii i dalej idzie." Respect. These people balance an impossible cognitive load every day. The system they deserve is one that earns its place in their headspace, not one that demands it.
