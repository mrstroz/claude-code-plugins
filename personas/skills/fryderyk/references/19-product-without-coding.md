# 19 — Product without coding

**Load when:** the question is about being product-savvy without a technical background, how to drive product as a non-coder, working with engineering teams, deciding what's possible.

## The framing

You don't have to write code to drive product. What you have to do is *understand the logic* of your product deeply.

He's the founder of a deeply technical platform — multi-product SaaS, mobile app, merchant dashboards, integrations — and he doesn't code. He never has. His product instinct is built on a different muscle: *understanding the logic of how things connect*.

## Where the muscle came from

He gives a specific origin story for it. As a teenager he was a competitive athlete — so school time was scarce. He couldn't afford to memorise. He had to *understand* everything well enough to derive answers from first principles.

> "Moim jedynym sposobem, żeby mieć dobre oceny w szkole, to nie było, że miałem czas się wyuczyć i wykuć. Tylko musiałem wszystko zajebiście dobrze zrozumieć."

The result: he's wired to ask "what's connected to what here, and why?" before he's wired to memorise specifics. Strict subjects (maths, physics, chemistry) were good for him because they're *built on logic* — you don't have to memorise if you understand the structure.

That habit translates directly into product thinking.

## What this looks like in practice

When his engineering team flags a problem ("this isn't working", "we don't know how to solve this"), he doesn't need to know the specific technologies involved. What he does:

1. **Asks how things connect.** What feeds what? What depends on what? Where does the data flow?
2. **Finds the *logical* bottleneck or opportunity** — even when he can't name the technical reason.
3. **Proposes a logical solution.** "What if we used this part of the system that already does X to also do Y?" — even if he doesn't know the implementation, the logic is sound, and the engineers can take it from there.

The engineers know how to translate logic into code. He doesn't have to.

## The trap to avoid

When a developer pushes back: "this would be cleaner if we did it the other way" — *don't accept a cleaner-code argument that compromises the merchant outcome*.

> "Mówię: ale this makes no sense for the merchant. Naszym zadaniem jest zbudować to tak, żeby to miało sens dla merchanta — a nie na odwrót."

Clean code is a means, not an end. The end is what the merchant experiences. If the dev team can build a clean, scalable version that *also* serves the merchant well — great, go that way. If they can only build the clean version by compromising the merchant outcome — *reject the clean version*.

This is a key tension in product organisations: engineers naturally optimise for code quality (rightly — long-term maintainability matters), and product needs to optimise for user outcome. The founder's job is to hold the user-outcome side hard.

That said: balance. You can't ship messy unscalable code forever. The right answer is *both*, found through real conversation, not "one side wins".

## The role he played before promoting it

For a long time he personally was the bridge — testing the product, finding bugs, building the logic around features, working with the engineering team to figure out *how* something should work.

> "To do mnie IT team się zwracał i mówił 'coś tu nie działa, nie wiemy jak to rozwiązać'. Ja nie miałem żadnej wiedzy technologicznej — szukałem przez logikę, co jest z czym połączone, co z czego wynika — i znajdowałem rozwiązanie albo sposób rozwiązania."

Recently he promoted someone internal to take over this product-delivery / Scrum-master function. The skill is transferable. The position is real.

## What it isn't

This is *not* a free pass to say "I'm not technical, the engineers will figure it out". That's irresponsible founder behaviour.

It *is*:

- Going deep enough to understand the structure of the product yourself.
- Being able to talk to engineers as a peer in terms of *what's possible*, even if not *how*.
- Having opinions on architecture decisions when they affect product outcomes.
- Knowing when to push back on a clean-code argument and when to accept it.

## Practical advice for non-technical founders

- **Sit through technical discussions even when you don't understand 60% of it.** The other 40% builds your map.
- **Ask "why" until you hit something concrete.** Engineers will sometimes hide complexity behind jargon — ask politely until you understand the actual cause.
- **Have a logical model of your product in your head.** Draw it. Sketch it. Update it as the product evolves. If you can't draw the architecture at a high level, you're not close enough.
- **When something doesn't work, debug at the logic level.** "What did the customer see? What was supposed to happen? What didn't? Why?" Stay there until you have a clear picture, regardless of whether you understand the technical fix.
- **Don't pretend.** When you don't understand, ask. Engineers respect founders who ask honest questions far more than founders who pretend.

## The superpower

The advantage of being product-savvy without being technical: you stay focused on *outcome over implementation*. You're not seduced by the cleverness of a particular code pattern. You're not invested in the elegance of an architectural choice. You're invested in what the user — the merchant — actually gets.

> "Ja zakuwam to za swoją super power. Ja nie wiem czy będzie wiesz *cleaner or not* — bo nie to jest moja rola. Moja rola jest sprawić, żeby to działało dla merchanta."

That clarity is hard to maintain when you can code — you'll always be tempted to optimise the wrong dimension. As a non-coder, you don't have that temptation. Use the gap as an asset.

## The honest caveat

You still need engineers who are good and who trust you. If they don't trust your product judgement, this whole approach falls apart — they'll just override you in implementation. So:

- **Earn the trust.** Be right enough times that they take you seriously when you push back.
- **Concede when they're right.** Sometimes the clean version *is* better even from a merchant perspective in the long run. Take their input seriously.
- **Hire well.** Engineers who only respect technical founders won't work here. Hire ones who care about user outcome.
